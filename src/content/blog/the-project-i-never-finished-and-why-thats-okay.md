## The Dream

I wanted to build a full student portal: attendance, grades, messaging, and a chat system. All in one summer.

I designed the database first. Beautiful ER diagram. Normalized to 3NF.

Then I started coding.

## The Slow Death

Week 1: User login worked.  
Week 2: Attendance tracking worked (mostly).  
Week 3: Grades module? Half done.  
Week 4: Messaging? I realized I needed WebSockets. Didn't know them yet.  
Week 5: Burnout. Overwhelm. The folder sat untouched.

## The Admission

I never finished that portal. Not even close.

For months, I felt like a failure. Every time someone asked "what are you building?", I changed the subject. My GitHub had a repo with a green checkmark on the README and nothing working underneath.

Then I talked to a senior developer at a campus event. I told them about the portal — the ambition, the crash, the silence.

They laughed — kindly — and said: *"That's three months of full‑time work for a team of three. You're one student. Of course you didn't finish."*

## What I Actually Gained

That conversation changed how I see unfinished work. Because I didn't walk away empty-handed:

- A database schema I have reused for two other smaller projects
- Experience with authentication (bcrypt, sessions, password hashing)
- The knowledge that WebSockets exist (and a note pinned in my notebook to learn them properly)
- A real understanding of scope — not from a textbook, but from bleeding

## The New Rule

Now I ask before starting any project: *"What is the smallest version that works?"*

Not the dream version. Not the "impress everyone" version. The *works* version.

One feature. One user flow. One clear job to be done.

If that works, I can add more. But I never start with "more" anymore.

## Why I Don't Hide That Repo

That unfinished portal sits in my GitHub repositories. It's not private. I don't delete it.

Why? Because it's honest. It shows someone who tried something hard, learned where the limits were, and grew from it.

Every recruiter or fellow developer who looks at my GitHub will see that repo. And that's fine. Because next to it, they'll see smaller projects — finished ones — built with the lessons that portal taught me.

## The Truth About Students

We are told to finish everything we start. To have a perfect portfolio. To never show failure.

That's not real.

Real is: you dream big, you crash, you get up, and you build something smaller that actually works.

That unfinished portal? It's not a tombstone. It's a lesson board. And I'm glad I built it.
